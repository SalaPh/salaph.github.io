About Me

I am a Swiss cosmologist and physicist with a varied spectrum of scientific interests. I was born and raised in the Italian side of Switzerland and grew up very fond of the outdoors, which made me curious about the surrounding world and the forces of nature but also led me to enjoy spending time in the mountains, hiking and skiing.

Currently in the final stretch of my PhD at the Institute for Theoretical Particle Physics and Cosmology (TTK) at RWTH Aachen, my research sits at the intersection of gravitational wave (GW) phenomenology and cosmology. I am convinced that the rapidly growing field of GW astronomy will be revolutionary for the understanding of our Universe, and I am happy to be part of this effort. In my work, I like to take an observational perspective, referencing to current and forthcoming experiments. My most recent projects featured forecasts on GW measurements with MAGIS, LVK and ET, and studied their impact in advancing our understanding astrophysical processes and improving cosmological inference.

After obtaining a Bachelor degrees in Physics at ETH Zurich (2020), I moved to Lausanne to pursue a more interdisciplinary Master at EPFL (2023), with major in theoretical physics, combined with a minor in space technologies. During my studies I also spent a semester abroad at the University of Toronto, and wrote my thesis at the University of Geneva with Prof. Antonio Riotto. The next step led to me Aachen, for a PhD in cosmology supervised by Dr. Sebastian Baum and Prof. Julien Lesgourgues.

I am also passionate about more applied sides of physics, especially climate sciences geophysics and space research and industry, about which I like to keep myself updated and expand my knowledge through a variety of readings. Furthermore, I care deeply about fighting climate change and preserving the health of our planet.

In my free time I have several hobbies. During the good season I enjoy hiking, explore diverse expressions of photography and take a swim in every lake, river or sea I encounter, while the rest of the year I keep busy with different winter sports, from skiing to ice hockey. In addition, I never say no to a good time paired with a nice barbecue, cheese, or wine.

Feel free to navigate my website and check out my linked CV for more details about myself.

Research

Cosmological parameter inference

The observation of our cosmos is rapidly advancing, and so are the techniques to infer the fundamental parameters of cosmology. A very powerful tool for this objective is the cross-correlation of different probes, which is free of many systematic effects and can be used to break degeneracies between parameters. One of my current research objectives is to enhance cross-correlation techniques, by correlating a multitude of cosmological probes, and with a particular focus on studying the value added by future gravitational wave (GW) catalogues. In a recent paper JCAP 05 (2026) 095, I explore the potential of the next generation of laser interferometers in observing compact binary coalescences (CBCs) to infer the Hubble constant, by comparing two complementary measurements of matter overdensities: one from galaxy cluster surveys, the other from forecasted GW catalogues.

White dwarf binaries with gravitational waves

While binary neutron stars (BNSs) and binary black holes (BBHs) have been studied in depth and already measured with GWs detectors, white dwarf binaries (WDBs) are more mysterious and elusive compact objects that are often overlooked in the GWs community, but equally exciting. Atom interferometry is an innovative quantum technology for GW astronomy being currently developed, that will provide the sensitivity required to observe individual WDB mergers. My recent work Phys. Rev. D 113 (2026) 123022, studies the possibility of resolving WDBs' GW signature with these instruments. The current sensitivity estimations are very promising, anticipating many yearly detections of WDB mergers and their consequent, visible, final events, opening a new window on multi-messenger astronomy, and providing valuable insights on the origin of Type Ia Supernovae.

Stochastic gravitational wave background

The fact that we can detect GW is already a pretty extraordinary result. The idea that we might be able to observe GW produced in an infant Universe is totally mind-blowing. It seems that the detection of a stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) is within reach of the forthcoming detectors, accompanied by the challenge of disentangling astrophysical and cosmological contributions to the signal. I am very interested in getting involved in this effort and eager to contribute to the GW community's push towards the first detection of the SGWB. Currently, I am investigating methods to overcome this obstacle in the LISA frequency band, with particular focus on studying and reconstructing the anisotropies of the different components of the SGWB.

Primordial black holes phenomenology

Primordial black holes (PBHs) are an exciting candidate for dark matter. During my Master's thesis, written at the University of Geneva, I studied the implications of having a presence of PBHs at high redshifts and the possibility that their clustering could influence early galaxy formation, as observed by James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). PBHs are an active field of research that I am very fond of, and I am willing to further explore it in future works.

Other interests

Space

The same curiosity that drives my research in cosmology and astrophysics has always also fuelled my interest in space exploration and technologies. For my Master, I specifically moved to Lausanne to be able to pursue both my major in physics and a minor in space technologies, unique in Switzerland at the time. This programme gave me the chance to learn about system engineering, mission and spacecraft design, and aerospace engineering. In addition, I complemented this knowledge with hands-on experience in the student-led rocket team, both at ETH and EPFL, and the spacecraft team at EPFL, where I had the opportunity to work with companies and celebrities of the space sector, like Prof. Claude Nicollier, the first Swiss astronaut. While my work now focuses on the more theoretical side of space, I remain very interested in aerospace field, and ready to jump on any opportunity that might combine multiple interests.

Geophysics and climate sciences

I have always been very passionate about anything nature related: atmospheric phenomena, geological processes, planetary science, flora and fauna, etc. which I enjoy exploring on a recreational level, as I never had the chance to study it academically. Nevertheless, during my civil service, I aquired some experience in solar physics, by serving a few months at IRSOL. At the same time, I am scientifically curious about any physical processes of the Universe, from cosmological to planetary and ecosystem scales. Geophysics and climate sciences particularly interest me, as they sit at the intersection of my physics background and personal interests, and I am always eager to engage with these topics and learn more through independent reading.
